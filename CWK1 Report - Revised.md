### Q2. The Belbin roles of every team member

According to Belbin's Team Roles theory, effective teams are formed through a balance of complementary behavioural contributions rather than technical ability alone (Belbin, 2010). In our team, **Siyuan Jiang** acts as the **Chair (CH)**. The Chair's function is to clarify objectives, coordinate contributions, and allocate work appropriately, which corresponds to Belbin's description of the role as a calm and objective coordinator who ensures that team resources are used effectively (Belbin, 2010). In our project context, Siyuan leads planning discussions, organises meetings, and ensures that responsibilities are clearly distributed.

**Jiahui Zeng** is the **Plant (PL)**. Belbin identifies the Plant as the role most associated with originality and idea generation, especially when a team needs novel solutions to complex problems (Belbin, 2010). Jiahui contributes primarily through proposing innovative features for scooter booking and location tracking, and through shaping user interface concepts that improve usability.

**Yuhao Lin** fulfils the **Monitor/Evaluator (ME)** role. This role is defined by critical judgement, logical reasoning, and impartial assessment of alternatives (Belbin, 2010). Yuhao evaluates our architecture, database choices, and testing strategy to ensure that key decisions are realistic and technically sound.

Finally, **Haozhe Peng** is the **Company Worker (CW)**, also commonly referred to as the Implementer in Belbin's framework. Belbin describes this role as disciplined and practical, with a focus on converting ideas into concrete, workable outputs (Belbin, 2010). Haozhe translates team decisions into implementation by developing core functionality and database models with attention to detail.

### Q3. Team strengths (3 points)

Our core strength is the way our existing Belbin role combination supports the technical requirements of an electric scooter rental system. The **Plant** contributes creative feature ideas, while the **Company Worker** transforms those ideas into reliable implementation, particularly in backend and database development. The **Chair** maintains coordination and alignment across tasks, and the **Monitor/Evaluator** provides rigorous scrutiny of design and testing decisions. This complementary arrangement supports both innovation and quality control, enabling the team to produce solutions that are not only original but also feasible and robust (Belbin, 2010). Therefore, our principal strength is not a single individual skill, but a role-based collaboration structure that links idea generation, coordination, critical evaluation, and execution.

### Q4. Team weaknesses (3 points)

A key behavioural weakness in our team is the absence of both a **Shaper (SH)** and a **Team Worker (TW)**. Belbin (2010) characterises the Shaper as the role that drives urgency, sustains momentum under pressure, and challenges complacency when progress slows. Without this role, the team may struggle to maintain pace during demanding periods, especially near sprint deadlines. At the same time, Belbin describes the Team Worker as the role that supports interpersonal cohesion, mediates tensions, and helps maintain collaborative morale (Belbin, 2010). Without a Team Worker, communication gaps or emotional friction may remain unresolved for longer than is healthy for team performance.

This gap is particularly significant in our role composition because the **Monitor/Evaluator's** necessary critical feedback may unintentionally discourage the **Plant** or frustrate the **Company Worker** when not moderated through a diplomatic team-maintenance role. Consequently, the **Chair** may carry a disproportionate dual burden: both maintaining delivery momentum (a Shaper-like function) and preserving morale/relationship stability (a Team Worker-like function). Over time, this concentration of pressure on the Chair may reduce team resilience and increase coordination risk.

### Q5. Mitigation strategy (4 points)

To mitigate the missing **Shaper**, the **Chair** will institutionalise short-interval accountability within our existing bi-weekly status meetings. Instead of broad progress updates only, each meeting will define narrowly scoped, time-bound checkpoints for every member, and completion of these checkpoints will be reviewed at the next session. This mechanism is designed to create constructive performance pressure and maintain continuity of progress between meetings, thereby reproducing the forward-driving effect that a Shaper would usually provide.

To mitigate the missing **Team Worker**, we will adopt a structured, blameless conflict-resolution protocol during Sprint Retrospectives. When disagreement occurs, the team will first frame the issue in neutral, evidence-based terms, then allow each member to explain perspective and constraints, and finally agree on specific improvement actions without personal attribution of fault. This protocol is intended to handle interpersonal friction objectively and preserve psychological safety, particularly when the **Monitor/Evaluator's** critiques are directed at ideas proposed by the **Plant** or implementation choices made by the **Company Worker**. In this way, team cohesion is protected through process design, even in the absence of a naturally relational Team Worker role.
